What is this?

At CERN, a proton beam flies almost a kilometre through a vacuum tube, from one accelerator to the experiments. 22 small “corrector” magnets keep it on track: 12 push it up or down, 10 push it left or right. After every maintenance break their settings are slightly off, and the beam crashes into the wall. Operators then spend hours re-steering it by hand, mostly blind, because once the beam is lost, every sensor after that point goes dark.

This website is a simulation of that problem, built as a test for AI agents. The agent sees only what an operator sees: 21 sensors, each made of two thin metal plates that the beam passes through. If both plates get the same amount of beam, it’s centred. Each try (“shot”) gives only a small, noisy clue, and the true magnet errors stay hidden.

Why it matters: AI is starting to be trusted with real scientific work. This task can’t be solved by guessing or by recalling an answer: you have to combine noisy clues over many shots and reason about what you can’t see. A dedicated statistical algorithm centres the beam to 67% on average; Gemini, one of today’s best AI models, only to 29%.

Why the goal is 95%, not 100%: the sensors are noisy, and the real machine never matches its blueprint exactly, so perfect centring isn’t reachable. Above about 95% the beam passes the whole line cleanly, which is good enough for the experiments. 95% is the “solved” threshold of the CERN study this project builds on.

Technical details

Machine: a simulated CERN transfer line (900 m, 400 GeV protons), with 22 corrector magnets (12 vertical = M1–M12, 10 horizontal = M13–M22) and 21 split-plate sensors. The simulation includes sensor noise and a slightly perturbed real machine compared with the blueprint the agent receives. It’s based on a published CERN study on beam steering (A. Štafa, 2026).

What you see on the plot: the tube is the pipe wall, and the orange line is the true beam path (never shown to the agent, see “AI view”). The arrows show how hard each magnet pushes; the hidden errors are not drawn.

Sensor rings: the white dot shows how far the beam passes from the centre, measured in beam widths. Inside the small circle = centred (green); inside the ring = off-centre (yellow); outside = far off.

Centring %: the average centring over all 21 sensors.

Benchmark score: in the official evaluation each run also gets a score from 0 to 1 (Algorithm 0.50, Gemini 0.26). It’s computed from the same centring; see the README.

Recorded runs: the website replays recorded runs of both players (one per puzzle for the algorithm, two per puzzle for Gemini), which is why pause and speed work. “This puzzle” on the Gemini card is the mean of its runs on that puzzle.

Play mode: you steer the 12 up–down magnets; the left–right ones are already set for you. For a fair comparison, switch on AI view: you start from zero magnets, see only the noisy sensors, and your result is shown when you switch AI view off.

Who plays

The algorithm: a Bayesian particle filter. It keeps 100,000 guesses about the hidden magnet errors and updates them after every shot. It uses exactly the same information and controls as the AI.

The AI: Gemini 3.8 Flash, run as an autonomous agent in a general-purpose coding harness (OpenCode via Harbor). It has a shell, can write and run its own code, and has the four beamline tools. On the “Watch Gemini” tab you can read its full reasoning in the reasoning panel, step by step.

Room to improve, on both sides: Gemini might do better with a task-specific harness, e.g. tools built just for steering the beam instead of a general coding setup (though then the tool does part of the reasoning). And the algorithm isn’t the ceiling either: it reaches about 67%, not the 95% goal, and more advanced methods, such as a trained reinforcement-learning policy, could steer better.
